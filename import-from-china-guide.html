How to import from China in 9 steps

China exported US$3.77 trillion of goods in 2025. Most of the money importers lose comes from a few avoidable mistakes. This playbook takes you from idea to delivered stock.

1. Validate the product and the numbers

Before you contact a factory, prove that demand exists and that the unit economics work. Look at marketplace sales estimates, search volume and competitor reviews (the 1–3 star reviews are a list of product defects to fix). Then run the profit calculator: if net margin after fees and ads is under 20%, rethink the product or the price.

2. Check regulations, certifications and duty

Find the product's HS code and look up your country's duty rate and any extra tariffs. Some products from China carry additional duties on top of normal rates. Check the required certifications: CE/UKCA (Europe/UK), FCC and UL/ETL for electronics (US), CPC for children's products, FDA registration for food-contact and cosmetics. Plug the duty rates into the landed cost calculator.

Rule of thumb: if you can't name the certification your product needs, you aren't ready to place an order.

3. Find suppliers

  • B2B marketplaces: large directories of manufacturers and trading companies. Filter for verified or audited suppliers.
  • Trade fairs: the Canton Fair (Guangzhou, three phases each spring and autumn), plus specialist fairs in Shenzhen, Shanghai and Yiwu. See the calendar.
  • Industrial clusters: Shenzhen (electronics), Yiwu (small commodities), Ningbo (appliances), Foshan (furniture), Guangzhou (apparel, beauty), Dongguan (manufacturing).
  • Sourcing agents: on-the-ground specialists who shortlist, negotiate and manage factories. See what they cost.

4. Vet suppliers (factory or trading company?)

Ask for the business licence and check the registered scope and capital. Request a video call walking the production line, and check export history (customs-record search tools show who they ship to). Trading companies aren't bad: they're often better for small or mixed orders. They just shouldn't pretend to be the factory.

5. Order samples (from two or three suppliers)

Expect to pay for samples and express shipping. Compare side by side, then approve a golden sample that both sides sign and keep. It becomes the quality reference for inspection.

6. Negotiate and put it in writing

Negotiate on more than price: MOQ, tiered pricing, packaging, lead time, payment terms and defect allowance. Your purchase agreement should list specs, the approved sample, the inspection standard (AQL), delivery date, penalties for delay, and IP clauses. Use an NNN agreement (non-use, non-disclosure, non-circumvention) for custom designs.

7. Pay safely

The standard is 30% deposit and 70% balance after a passed inspection and before loading. Use trade-assurance or escrow on platforms, or a letter of credit for large orders. Confirm bank details by phone or video before the first payment.

8. Inspect before you pay the balance

A pre-shipment inspection (PSI) by an independent third party usually costs a few hundred dollars per person-day. It is the cheapest insurance you'll buy. For new suppliers or complex products, add a during-production inspection (DUPRO). Book an inspection quote →

9. Ship, clear customs and receive

Choose an Incoterm (FOB is the most common; DDP is simplest for beginners), then a mode: express for samples, air for urgent launch stock, sea LCL or FCL for volume. Use the freight estimator to compare. A customs broker files your entry. Make sure the commercial invoice, packing list and bill of lading match exactly.

FAQ

How much money do I need to start importing from China?

Many first orders land between US$2,000 and US$10,000 including samples, inspection and freight. Low-MOQ suppliers and trading companies can go lower.

Do I need an import licence?

Most countries don't require a general licence for commercial goods, but you need a business/importer number (for example an EORI in the EU/UK, or a Business Number with an import account in Canada) and any product-specific permits.

How long does it take?

Typically 6–12 weeks: 1–2 weeks for samples, 3–6 weeks of production, then 1 week by air or 4–6 weeks by sea, door to door. Add 3–6 weeks around Chinese New Year.
